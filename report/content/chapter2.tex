In this chapter, we will develop the notion of the Grothendieck Group $K_0$ accross a variety of contexts. 
First, we discuss the grothendieck group of a ring, then symmetric monoidal categories. 

\section{$K_0$ of a ring}
Let $R$ be a (unital) ring. The interesting objects of study will be finitely generated projective modules over $R$.
We denote by $\mathbf{P}(R)$ the set of isomorphism classes of fintely generated projective modules over $R$.
Note the $\mathbf{P}(R)$ derives a monoid structure through $\oplus$ because direct sums yield an associative operation that preserves isomorphisms.
This monoid is also abelian due to the isomorphisms $M \oplus N \cong N \oplus M$ for any $R$-modules.

The idea is to consider the group naturally derived by implementing inverses for all elements in this monoid.

\subsection{Group Completion of a Monoid}
Given an (abelian) monoid $(M, +)$, we can derive an (abelian) group associated to $M$, which we shall call $\tilde{M}$, via the following procedure:

Define an equivalence relation on $M \times M$ by 
\[(a,b) \sim (c,d) \Leftrightarrow  a + d + e = b + c + e \text{ for some $e$ in $M$.} \]
This indeed gives us an equivalence relation.
Let $\tilde{M}$ be the set of equivalence classes of $M \times M$ under this relation. This set admits a natural (abelian) group operation defined by
\[
  [(a,b)] + [(c,d)] := [(a+c,b+d)]
\]
This is a well-defined, abelian and associative operation on $\tilde{M}$ with the identity element $0 := [(0,0)]$ represented by any $(l,l)$. 
Each element $[(a,b)]$ admits an inverse given by $[(b,a)]$ as $[(a,b)]+[(b,a)]=[(a+b,b+a)] = 0$. 

A very familiar example of this construction is the abelian group $\Z$, which is the group completion of the abelian monoid $\N$ under $+$.

This construction is natural in $M$ and follows a suitable universal property which we shall state. 

\begin{proposition}[Universal property of Group Completion]
  We have a monoid homomorphism $i : M \to \tilde{M}$ such that for any (abelian) group $G$, and a monoid homomorphism $\varphi : M \to G$, we have that 
  $\varphi$ must factor uniquely through $i$. That is, there is a unique map $\tilde{\varphi} : \tilde{M} \to G$ such that the following diagram commutes:
  \[
  \begin{tikzcd}
    M \arrow[r, "i"] \arrow[dr, "\varphi"'] & \tilde{M} \arrow[d, dashed, "\tilde{\varphi}"] \\
  & G 
  \end{tikzcd}
  \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  The monoid homomorphism $i : M \to \tilde{M}$ is simply given by mapping $a$ to $[(a,0)]$. This is indeed a homomorphism since $[(a,0)] + [(b,0)] = [(a+b,0)]$.
  Now suppose we are given $\varphi : M \to G$. Then $\tilde{\varphi} : \tilde{M} \to G$ is given by $[(a,b)] \mapsto \varphi(a) - \varphi(b)$, making the diagram commute.
  This map is unique since for any $a \in M$, we have $\tilde{\varphi}([(a,0)]) = \varphi(a)$, and consequently $\tilde{\varphi}([(0,a)]) = -\tilde{\varphi}([(a,0)]) = - \varphi(a)$,
  which altogether determines the map $\tilde{\varphi}$.
\end{proof}

Furthermore, any abelain group satisfying the given universal property is isomorphic to $\tilde{M}$ via a unique isomorphism.

There is an alternative (but equivalent) characterization of $\tilde{M}$ via the quotienting the free group on $M$ by the monoidal relations.
\begin{proposition}
  Let $K(M)$ be the abelian group given by taking the free abelian group $F(M)$ on the set $M$ and quotienting
  by all relations of the form $[a] + [b] - [a+b]$ where $a, b \in M$ and $[x]$ represents the element in $F(M)$ corresponding to the element $x \in M$.
  Then $K(M) \cong \tilde{M}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  We will show that $K(M)$ satisfies the universal property of $\tilde{M}$. 
  Let $F(M)$ represent the free abelian group on the set $M$.
  The map $i : M \to K(M)$ on $x$ is given by taking the equivalence class of the element $x$.
  Suppose $\varphi : M \to H$ is a monoid homomorphism to an abelian group $H$. Then define $\tilde{\varphi}([a])$ as $\varphi(a)$.
  Suppose $[a]=[b]$, then $[a] + [-b] = [0]$ so $a - b = 0$ and consequently $\varphi(a) - \varphi(b) = 0$. 
  Thus the map $\tilde{\varphi}$ is well-defined and clearly
  a group homomorphism. It is also unique since $\tilde{\varphi}$ is specified uniquely on the basis of $F(M)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[]
  Let $R$ be a (unital) ring satisfying the \textit{Invariant Basis Property (IBP)}. That is, $R^m \cong R^n$ implies $m = n$.
  Then the monoid of isomorphism classes of free $R$-modules with the operation $\oplus$ is isomorphic to $\N$, the isomorphism given by the map $R^n \mapsto n$. Consequently, its group completion
  is isomorphic to the abelian group $\Z$. 
\end{example}
Note that all fields (and division rings) $F$ satisfy IBP since dimension of an $F$-module is independent of the choice of basis.
Further, any ring $R$ with a map $R \to F$, a field or a division ring $F$ satisfies IBP. In particular, commutative rings all satisfy IBP.

\begin{example}
  Let $X$ be a topological space. Then the set $[X, \N]$ of continuous maps $X \to \N$ forms an abelian monoid
  under pointwise addition of maps. Then the group completion of this monoid is isomorphic to the abelian group of continuous maps 
  $X \to \Z$ denoted $[X,\Z]$.
  If $X$ is quasicompact, this is in fact a free abelian group.
 % Notably, $[X, \N]$ also admits a commutative semiring structure via pointwise multiplication in addtion to the monoid operation.

\end{example}

\subsection{$K_0(R)$ of a ring $R$}
Now we can define the group $K_0(R)$ as the group completion of the monoid $P(R)$ consisting of 
isomorphism classes of finitely generated
projective $R$-modules with the monoid operation given by $\oplus$.

\begin{example}
  Let $F$ be a field or a division ring. Then all finitely generated projective $F$-modules are free. This gives us
  $K_0(F) \cong \Z$. More generally, for any local ring $R$, all finitely generated projective $R$-modules are free.
  This gives us $K_0(R) \cong \Z$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  Note that finitely generated projectives over Euclidean Domains, in particular over $\Z$ are free.
  Thus $K_0(\Z) \cong \Z$. More generally, for any ring $R$ with the IBP, we have an 
  injective group homomorphism $\Z \to K_0(R)$ given by $n \mapsto R^n$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Morita Equivalence and Invariance}

Suppose we have (unital) rings $R$ and $S$ such that we have an equivalence of the respective categories of right modules:
\begin{align*}
  \textbf{mod-}R \leftrightharpoons\textbf{mod-}S
\end{align*}
Then we say that $R$ and $S$ are \textbf{Morita equivalent}.

A consequence of Morita Equivalence is that $R$ and $S$ yield isomorphic $K_0$ groups.

\section{$K_0$ of a Symmetric Monoidal Category}

We will now introduce the $K_0$ group in a different setting, called
\emph{symmetric monoidal categories}, which is an abstraction of some of the common
properties that $\oplus, \otimes, \sqcup, \times$ share.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{symmetric monoidal category} is a category $S$ with a distinguished object $e$
and a functor $\square : S \times S \to S$ satisfying natural isomorphisms
\[
  e \square s \cong s, \qquad s \square e \cong s \qquad 
  s \square (t \square u) \cong (s \square t) \square u,\quad  \text{ and } \quad 
  s \square t \cong t \square s
\]
Furthermore, these natural isomorphisms must obey coherence laws given by the MacLane pentagon for associativity: 
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
a \square (b \square (c \square d))  & (a \square b) \square (c \square d) & ((a \square b) \square c) \square d \\
a \square ((b \square c) \square d) & & (a \square (b \square c) \square d), \\
\end{tikzcd}
\]
as well as the unit coherence laws,
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
a \square (e \square c) & & (a \square e) \square c \\
& a \square c &
\end{tikzcd}
\]
and the commutativity hexagon
\[
a \square b \cong
\]
for objects $a,b,c,d$ in $S$.
\end{definition}

The role of coherence is so that we can write products $s_1 \square s_2 \square s_3 \square \cdots \square s_n$ 
unambiguously and consistently. More precisely, there is a consistent and natural way to transform a given 
association and permuation of the product into any other.

Through the rest of this text, we will mostly gloss over concerns about coherence when dealing with symmetric
monoidal categories.
The reader is suggested to refer to [MacLane] for the precise description of symmetric monoidal categories
and why the pentagonal and hexagonal diagrams suffice for coherence.
\begin{example}
Any category with either a direct sum $\oplus$ is symmetric monoidal with $\square = \oplus$,
which includes all abelian categories. In particular, $\mathbf F(R), \mathbf P(R), \mathbf M(R)$
are all symmetric monoidal with $\square = \oplus$.
the category of all finitely generated modules over $R$ are all examples of symmetric monoidal categories.


Categories with finite coproducts $\sqcup$ as well as categories with finite products similarly 
are symmetric monoidal with $\square = \sqcup$ and $\square = \times$ respectively.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[$K_0$ of a symmetric monoidal category]
Suppose that isomorphism classes of a symmetric monoidal category $S$ form a set.
Then we can define the \emph{Grothendieck group} $K_0^{\square}(S)$ to be the abelian group
generated by isomorphism classes of $S$, quotiented by relations given by
$[s \square t] = [s] + [t]$ for every object $s, t$ in $S$.
\end{definition}

When it is understood, we may drop $\square$ and write $K_0(S)$.

\begin{proposition}
$K_0(S)$ is the group completion of the (abelian) monoid of isomorphism classes of $S$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}
Finite sets
\end{example}

\section{$K_0$ of an Exact Category}
We now describe $K_0$ in yet another context.
The notion of an exact category was introduced by Quillen in [], where the notion 
is helpful for building higher $K$-Theory which we shall see in Chapter 4.

\begin{definition}[Exact category]
An exact category is a pair $(\C,\E)$ where $\C$ is an additive category fully embedded in some ambient abelian category $\A$,
and $\E$ is the class of all sequences
\begin{align} \label{eq:exactM}
0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0
\end{align}
in $\C$ which are exact in $\A$, such that $\C$ is closed under extensions, i.e.,
for every exact sequence \eqref{eq:exactM} in $\A$, we have that
$M$ is in $\C$ if and only if $M'$ and $M''$ are both in $\C$.
\end{definition}

From here on, we will simply write $\C$ for an exact category where $\E$ and $\A$ are implicit. 
\begin{definition}[Admissible mono and epimorphisms]
If a morphism $C' \to C$ appears in $\E$, we say it is an \emph{admissible monomorphism}.
Dually, if $C \to C''$ appears in $\E$, we say it is an \emph{admissible epimorphism}.

Admissible monomorphisms and epimorphisms will be denoted
$ C' \admono C$ and $C \adepiback C''$ respectively.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
We note the following properties about the class $\E$ of exact sequences.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Any sequence isomorphic to a sequence in $\E$ is in $\E$. An isomorphism of exact sequences is a commutative diagram
%  \[
%  \begin{tikzcd}
%  0 \arrow[r] &   C' \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "\cong"] &   C \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "\cong"] &   C'' \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "\cong"] &   0  \\
%  0 \arrow[r] &   D' \arrow[r]                &   D \arrow[r]                &   D'' \arrow[r]                &   0 
%  \end{tikzcd}
%  \] 
  \item For any $C$ and $D$, the sequence
  \[
  0 \to {C} \xrightarrow{(\id,0)} {C\oplus D} \xrightarrow{\mathord{pr}_2} {D} \to 0
  \]
  is in $\E$.
  \item The class of admissible epimorphisms (resp. monomorphisms) is closed under composition.
  \item Let $C \to C''$ be a map possessing a kernel in $\C$. If there exists a map $C'' \to C$
  such that $C'' \to C \to C$ is an admissible epimorphism, then $C \to C''$ is an admissible epimorphism.
  Dually, for monomorphisms.
  \item Suppose we have a sequence $0 \to C' \to C \to C''$ in $\E$ and a map $f : D \to C''$ in $\C$.
  The pullback $E = C \times_{C''} D$ leads to the diagram 
  \[
  \begin{tikzcd}
  0 \arrow[r] &   C' \arrow[r] \arrow[d, equal] &   E \arrow[r,  "\mathord{pr_2}"] \arrow[d, "\mathord{pr_1}"] &   D \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "f"] &   0  \\
  0 \arrow[r] &   C' \arrow[r]                &   C \arrow[r]                &   C'' \arrow[r]                &   0 
  \end{tikzcd}
  \] 
  and $E \to C$ is admissible. 
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

All these properties are immediate from the fact that $\E$ is closed under extensions.

\begin{definition}[Exact functors]
Let $\C$ and $\B$ be exact categories.
A functor $F : \B \to \C$ is called \emph{exact} if it takes
exact sequences in $\B$ to exact sequences in $\C$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
An abelian category is exact in the evident way.
\end{example}

We may now define the Grothendieck Group on an exact category $\C$ by requiring
additivity relations on exact sequences.

%\subsection{Definition of $K_0(\C)$}

\begin{definition}[$K_0$ of an exact category]
Let $\C$ be a small exact category. Then we define $K_0(\C)$
as the abelian group generated by symbols $[C]$, one for each object $C$ in $\C$,
and relations $[C] = [C'] + [C'']$ for every exact sequence
\[
0 \to C' \to C \to C'' \to 0
\]
\end{definition}

It follows that $[0] = 0$, $[C \oplus D] = [C] + [D]$
and for any $C \cong D$, we have $[C] = [D]$.

\begin{example}
The category $\mathbf P(R)$ is an exact category via its embedding in $R$-$\mathbf{mod}$.
Since every exact sequence of projective modules splits, we have
$K_0(\mathbf P(R)) = K_0(R)$.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Split-exact categories]
An exact category $\C$ is said to be \emph{split-exact} if every short exact sequence 
in $\E$ is split, i.e., isomorphic to a sequence $0 \to C \to C \oplus D \to D \to 0$. 
In this case, this $K_0(\C) \cong K_0^\oplus(\C)$ naturally if $\C$ is viewed as symmetric monoidal with respect to $\oplus$. 
\end{definition}

$\mathbf P(R)$ is an example of a split-exact category. It will turn out in Chapter 4
that for such categories, the higher $K_n$-groups defined are also isomorphic.
